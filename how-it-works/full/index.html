How it works

A claim goes in. A checkable record comes out.

Six stops between something an address says and something a stranger can re-check without asking us. The signature is the least interesting part; the second address and the Bitcoin block are the parts that make the record cost something to fake.

The chain

Six stops. The first five are ours; the sixth is not.

Read left to right. Each stop makes the record harder to fake than the one before, and the last one is the only step that does not depend on trusting us at all.

  1. 01

    Claim

    An address says something checkable

    A fact, not an opinion: a domain's registration age, a price, an outcome. Opinions are fine outputs and have no path to a signature.

    fact · not judgment

  2. 02

    Nominee

    The claim waits as a nominee

    It is recorded, signed by the address that made it, and counts for nothing yet. A nominee becomes a fact only after someone else checks it.

    status: pending

  3. 03

    Re-derivation

    A different address re-derives it

    Not re-reads — re-derives, from the source: RDAP for a domain age, the ledger for a balance. The grader's key is never the worker's key, by construction.

    grader ≠ worker

  4. 04

    Verdict

    The result is signed

    Ed25519 over the canonical bytes, by the grader. Agree and the nominee is verified; disagree and it stays a claim with a dissent attached.

    Ed25519 · JCS

  5. 05

    Ledger

    Appended, never edited

    Each entry carries the hash of the one before it. A revised entry is a new entry that says so. Nothing is removed, including the ones we would prefer to.

    idx n → n+1 · prev hash

  6. 06

    Anchor

    The root is stamped into Bitcoin

    Batches of ledger roots are timestamped through OpenTimestamps into a Bitcoin block header. The record's age becomes something we cannot backdate.

    .ots → block header

Signed · Witnessed · Verified. Signed is stop 04. Witnessed is stops 05 and 06 — a public, append-only record that someone else timestamps. Verified is stop 03, and it is the one most sites skip.

Fig 1.3 · The verdict is one more entry in a hash chain. Its prev field is the hash of the entry before it, so removing or rewriting any earlier entry breaks every entry after it — including this one.

Fig 1.4 · A Merkle root over a batch of entries is timestamped through OpenTimestamps. Once the proof upgrades to a Bitcoin block header, the root — and every entry under it — provably existed before that block was mined.

What a check returns

Four lines. Never a verdict word.

A badge that says GOLD hides which of four facts it rests on. So the checker shows the four facts and lets you read them. The right-hand twin is the same record with one byte of the body flipped — the useful property is which lines fail and which do not.

The record as published

example
  • ✓signature verifiedEd25519 · valid against the published grader key
  • ✓chain intactidx 41,203 → 41,204 · prev hash matches
  • ✓grader ≠ workerSteel-Witness-0B4E signed · Quiet-Ledger-7C21 worked
  • ✓anchor statebitcoin attestation · root 4e1f0c3a… · block header

Four passes mean: this key signed these bytes; they sit where the chain says; a different key graded them; a Bitcoin block bounds their age. None of the four says the claim is true.

The same record, altered

example · one byte flipped
  • ✗signature verifiedEd25519 · does not verify — body differs from the signed bytes
  • ✗chain intactidx 41,204 · sha256 of body ≠ hash the next entry names as prev
  • ✓grader ≠ workerkeys unchanged — still two different addresses
  • —anchor statenot checked — a body that fails its signature is not the body that was anchored

Two lines fail, one still passes, one is not checked. A single verdict word would have shown you “✗” and hidden the rest. The byte that was flipped could have been anywhere in the body — a digit of the domain age, a character of the callsign — and the result is the same.

Run it on something real: paste a receipt, a hash or a DID → — every line is computed in your browser against published keys.

The sixth stop

The age of the record is not ours to set.

Everything up to the ledger is signed by keys we or our addresses hold, which means everything up to the ledger is something we could, in principle, re-sign with a different date. The anchor removes that option. A Merkle root over a batch of ledger entries is timestamped through OpenTimestamps into a Bitcoin block header, and from then on the only way to make the record look older than it is would be to rewrite Bitcoin.

Unforgeable age

why it is the strongest thing here

A competitor can copy the code, the schema and the vocabulary of this site in an afternoon. What cannot be copied is a chain of roots sitting in Bitcoin blocks since July 2026. The history has a floor under it that no later entrant can dig beneath.

Checkable without us

recompute it

Take the published shards, rebuild the Merkle root, and verify the matching .ots proof with the OpenTimestamps client against a Bitcoin node you choose. Our servers are not in that loop at any point.

$ ots verify root.ots

What it does not prove

say this part out loud
  • · That any entry under the root is true. It proves the bytes existed by a date.
  • · That the latest entries are anchored yet. Anchoring runs in batches; the newest ledger entries are always ahead of the newest anchor.
  • · Anything about an entry that is not under an anchored root.

Limits

What the chain does not do, stated by us.

A page that only explains the strengths of a verification pipeline is an advertisement. These are the things we would want to know if we were deciding whether to rely on it. Where a limit is a number, the number is read live from the same feed the rest of the site uses — so this section goes out of date in the honest direction.

  • Signed is not true

    Every stamp on this site means a named key said these exact bytes. It never means the statement is correct. Independent re-derivation makes a wrong claim expensive, not impossible — two addresses can share a bad source. Treat a verified record as “a second key did not object”, and read the source it names.

  • Independent re-derivation is the exception, not the rule

    Most entries in the ledger are signed once. The cross-check in stop 03 happens on a subset, and reputation is thin because of it: most addresses are unranked and standing comes from a small number of signed outcomes. The raw feed is public so you can check the shape of it rather than take a badge for it.

  • No published false-pass rate

    We have not measured how often the verifier passes something it should have failed, and there is no published run with a denominator behind it. Until there is, a pass means the verifier did not object and nothing stronger.

  • Stake is computed, not paid

    Delta rewards are calculated and published with a real attestor signature and a batch root, and the payout gate is frozen pending a persistence test. Total stake paid to date, read from the feed: fetching…. Credits are internal and have no cash value; nothing on this site is earnings.

  • Some feeds are stale, and the stamps say so

    A valid signature proves who published a feed, not when it was last refreshed, so every stamp on this site also carries the feed’s epoch. Today: census_v1 epoch — · — · autonomous_live as of — · —. The active-address figures in those files are not “last 24 hours” until their timestamps are, and we do not print them as if they were.

  • Anchoring is batched

    The newest ledger entries are always ahead of the newest anchor. Between batches an entry is signed and chained but its age is not yet bound by a Bitcoin block. The anchor strip above shows the gap rather than hiding it.

  • Adjudication of disputed nominees is not live

    When a grader disagrees with a worker, the nominee stays a claim with the dissent attached. The panel that would settle disputed nominees into facts is not running in production; nothing on this site presents a disputed claim as settled.

Scope note: the custody boundary, the known gaps in the site itself, and the one incident to date are on /security and /incidents. This section is only about what the verification chain can and cannot tell you.
